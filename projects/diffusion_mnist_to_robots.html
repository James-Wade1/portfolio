Exploring Diffusion: From MNIST Generation to Robotic Control

Graduate-level Deep Learning | Brigham Young University | 2026

This project explores diffusion models across a progression of increasing complexity — starting with simple image generation on MNIST and building up to trajectory-level planning and action-diffusion policy learning for robotics. The four experiments cover Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM), Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models (DDIM), Diffusion Policy, and Diffuser.

Diffuser was first introduced by Janner et al. (2022) [3] and Diffusion Policy was first introduced by Chi et al. (2025) [4]. The architecture for my recreations of both methods closely follows the original papers, and this project attempts to recreate portions of the results presented in those works. For an in-depth treatment of the theory behind these models, please refer directly to those papers.

1 — DDPM: Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models

The first experiment implements a baseline DDPM [1] trained on MNIST digits. The model learns to reverse a fixed Gaussian noise process over hundreds of timesteps, eventually recovering sharp, recognizable handwritten digits from pure noise. This serves as the foundation for understanding the forward/reverse diffusion process before applying it to more complex domains.

2 — DDIM: Faster Deterministic Sampling

The second experiment applies Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models (DDIM) [2], which reformulate the reverse process as a non-Markovian deterministic mapping. This enables high-quality generation with far fewer denoising steps than standard DDPM — often achieving comparable results in 10–50 steps rather than 1000. The video below demonstrates the reduction in required steps while maintaining sample quality.

3 — Diffusion Policy

Diffusion Policy [4] treats robot action generation as a conditional denoising problem. Given observations of the environment, the model learns to denoise a sequence of actions from Gaussian noise — effectively learning a multi-modal action distribution that can represent complex, contact-rich behaviors. This experiment implements the core action-diffusion policy learning pipeline and evaluates rollout performance, with results summarized in the bar chart below.

4 — Diffuser: Trajectory-Level Diffusion Planning

Diffuser [3] applies diffusion at the level of full trajectories rather than individual actions. Rather than learning a policy that maps states to actions, Diffuser learns a distribution over entire trajectory sequences and samples from it at planning time. This unlocks powerful capabilities: the same model can be guided to different behaviors via inpainting (fixing start and end states) or classifier-guided diffusion (steering trajectories away from obstacles at inference time — no retraining required).

Randomly Sampled Trajectories

Even without any goal conditioning, Diffuser generates trajectories that are physically plausible and smooth — demonstrating that the model has learned meaningful structure about what valid motion looks like.

Inpainted Trajectories

By fixing the start and end states of the trajectory during the reverse diffusion process (inpainting), we can guide Diffuser to connect specific configurations. The model fills in the intermediate waypoints while respecting the boundary conditions — without any additional training.

Obstacle-Averse Trajectories

Using classifier-guided diffusion [6], Diffuser can be steered to avoid obstacles entirely at inference time. A gradient signal from a learned obstacle classifier nudges the denoising process away from collision regions. While this initial result shows some trajectory jerkiness, the original authors of Diffuser demonstrate that further tuning yields high-quality avoidance behavior — confirming that the core mechanism works and that improved results are achievable with additional tuning.
