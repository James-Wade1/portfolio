Medical Pressure Mat

Global Engineering Outreach | Brigham Young University | 2023 – 2024

As part of BYU's Global Engineering Outreach (GEO) program, a fellow student and I partnered with a non-profit wheelchair clinic in Ecuador to design an affordable medical pressure mat, as shown in Figure 1. These mats are critical for prescribing appropriate seat cushions to high-risk users — such as those at risk of developing pressure sores. Commercial systems typically cost between $1,000 and $10,000, far beyond the clinic's budget. Our prototype reduced that cost to just $50 — a 20x to 200x price reduction. We then traveled to Ecuador to test the prototype in-situ and gathered feedback for future improvements.

This two-person project was developed alongside a separate three-person effort focused on creating a low-cost wheelchair cushion for the same clinic. Our work has since been continued by subsequent GEO teams as a standalone project, building on the foundation we established.

Technical Details

Hardware Design

Our pressure mat design was inspired by the work of Nathan Day and Dr. Marc Killpack on flexible force sensors [1]. We initially experimented with readily available hobbyist force-sensitive resistors (FSRs), such as these, but quickly realized that their individual size and wiring complexity would make large-scale sensing impractical.

Instead, we adopted a resistive fabric called Velostat, whose local resistance decreases with applied force. By applying a voltage to a known row and measuring the voltage drop at a specific column, we can calculate the instantaneous resistance at that point. With proper calibration, this resistance is translated into localized force measurements. Scanning across the matrix produces a pressure map at discrete resolution.

Figure 2 illustrates the internal construction of the pressure mat. A layer of Velostat is sandwiched between two pieces of water-resistant fabric to provide moisture protection. Strips of copper tape are applied to the inner surfaces of both fabric layers, arranged in perpendicular rows and columns. When assembled, these strips form a grid pattern across the Velostat. By applying voltage to a selected row and grounding a specific column, the resistance at their intersection can be measured. This principle is demonstrated with a miniature prototype in Figure 3.

Figure 4 shows the two inner layers of our full-scale prototype, each incorporating 16 strips of copper tape, providing a sensing resolution of 256 distinct measurement points.

Electrical Design

Figure 5 shows the assembled protoboard of our system. Selective voltage application to individual rows and columns was achieved using a demultiplexer-multiplexer (demux-mux) pair. The demultiplexer directs a single input signal to one of many possible outputs, while the multiplexer selects one of many inputs to pass through to a single output channel.

During development, we encountered issues with parasitic voltage draw and unintended current flow through demux channels that were intended to remain inactive. To resolve this, we integrated voltage buffer circuits to electrically isolate the demux outputs from the rest of the system, ensuring accurate voltage control across the sensor matrix.

Once testing the resistance at each location, the Arduino transferred this data to the base station computer running the pressure mat GUI (Graphical User Interface) over serial.

Software Design

After receiving resistance data from the Arduino, the base station's role was to convert these values into estimated forces using a pre-determined calibration curve. Figure 6 shows the first and second versions of the Graphical User Interface (GUI) developed to visualize this data.

Due to project time constraints, my coworker and I were unable to complete the calibration process needed to directly correlate resistance to applied force. This task was passed on to future teams. In the interim, the GUI displayed raw resistance values, which still served as a relative indicator of applied pressure.

Summary

In summary, my team and I successfully delivered a working prototype of a medical pressure mat at 20x to 200x less the cost — providing invaluable support to patient care at this remote non-profit clinic. This project was an incredible opportunity to directly impact lives, while honing sound product development practices and engineering skills.
